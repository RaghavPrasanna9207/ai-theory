# Training Deep Neural Networks

Chapter 9/10 built shallow-ish MLPs (a few hidden layers). A genuinely *deep* network — dozens or hundreds of layers — runs into a different class of problems: gradients that vanish or explode as they propagate backward, not enough labeled data to fill millions of parameters, painfully slow training, and a heightened risk of overfitting. This chapter goes through each problem in turn and the standard toolkit for fixing it.

## The Vanishing/Exploding Gradients Problems

*   Backpropagation's backward pass computes an error gradient for every parameter by working from the output layer back toward the input layer (see the ANN chapter). In deep networks, these gradients frequently **shrink as they travel backward** — by the time the signal reaches the earliest layers, the gradient is so close to zero that gradient descent barely moves those weights at all, and training stalls without ever reaching a good solution. This is the **vanishing gradients problem**.
*   The opposite can also happen: gradients **grow** at each layer instead of shrinking, until weight updates become enormous and training diverges outright. This is the **exploding gradients problem**, most commonly seen in recurrent neural networks (a later chapter).
*   Both are symptoms of a broader issue called **unstable gradients**: different layers in the same network end up learning at wildly different speeds, which historically was one of the main reasons deep networks were largely abandoned in the early 2000s.
*   **Diagnosis (Glorot & Bengio, 2010)**: the culprits were the once-standard combination of the **sigmoid activation function** plus a naive weight initialization scheme (random values from a normal distribution with mean 0, standard deviation 1). With this combination, the *variance* of each layer's output ends up much larger than the variance of its input. Stack many layers, and this variance keeps compounding layer after layer until, by the upper layers, the sigmoid function is **saturating** — pushed so far into its flat extremes (near 0 or near 1) that its derivative is nearly zero there. Since backprop's downward-flowing gradient signal is built directly from these derivatives, once it hits a saturated layer there's essentially nothing left to pass further down — the lower layers get almost no gradient at all. (Sigmoid's saturation is made slightly worse by the fact that its own output is centered at 0.5, not 0 — tanh, centered at 0, behaves a bit better for this reason, though it doesn't fully solve the problem.)

## Glorot Initialization and He Initialization

*   Glorot and Bengio's fix targets *both* directions signal has to flow cleanly through a layer: forward (making predictions) and backward (propagating gradients). Neither direction should shrink the signal to nothing or blow it up. Mathematically, this means wanting a layer's *output* variance to match its *input* variance, **and** wanting the gradient's variance to be preserved going backward through that same layer.
*   You can't perfectly guarantee both conditions unless a layer has exactly as many inputs as outputs (these counts are called the layer's **fan-in** and **fan-out**). Glorot and Bengio's practical compromise: initialize weights randomly using a variance based on the *average* of fan-in and fan-out, $fan_{avg} = (fan_{in}+fan_{out})/2$.
*   **Glorot (Xavier) initialization** (for sigmoid/tanh/softmax/no activation): draw weights from a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance $\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{fan_{avg}}$, or equivalently a uniform distribution between $-r$ and $+r$ with $r = \sqrt{\frac{3}{fan_{avg}}}$.
*   **LeCun initialization** is the same formula but using $fan_{in}$ in place of $fan_{avg}$ — it predates Glorot's paper by over a decade (Yann LeCun, 1990s) and is identical to Glorot initialization whenever $fan_{in} = fan_{out}$.
*   **He (Kaiming) initialization**, proposed later specifically for ReLU and its variants, uses a larger variance — $\sigma^2 = \frac{2}{fan_{in}}$ — since ReLU's flat zero region for negative inputs means signal needs a bigger boost to avoid shrinking on the way through. This is the initialization to reach for whenever you're using ReLU, Leaky ReLU, ELU, GELU, Swish, Mish, SwiGLU, or ReLU² (all covered below). For SELU specifically, use LeCun initialization (with a normal distribution).
*   **The takeaway that matters more than any specific formula**: *matching your initialization scheme to your activation function is not a minor detail — it was one of the concrete tricks that made training genuinely deep networks practical at all.* Using the wrong initialization scheme can leave a deep network struggling to train no matter how good everything else about the setup is.
*   **PyTorch specifics**: `nn.Linear` actually initializes with Kaiming uniform initialization by default, but — for historical/legacy reasons — with a scale factor that isn't quite right for any standard activation function. To get properly-scaled Kaiming initialization, either manually rescale the weights after creating the layer, or (cleaner) call the initializer functions in `torch.nn.init` directly: `nn.init.kaiming_uniform_(layer.weight)` and `nn.init.zeros_(layer.bias)` (there's generally no benefit to randomly initializing bias terms). To apply one initialization scheme across every `nn.Linear` layer in a model at once, write a small function that checks `isinstance(module, nn.Linear)` and applies the desired initializer, then run it over the whole model via `model.apply(your_init_function)` — this recurses into every submodule automatically.
*   PyTorch also provides `nn.init.orthogonal_()`, which initializes weights as a random **orthogonal matrix** — a matrix that provably preserves the norm (magnitude) of any vector it multiplies, which keeps variance stable through a layer at the start of training. It's less commonly used than Glorot/He/LeCun, but can help specifically in recurrent networks or generative adversarial networks (later chapters).
*   **A useful trick for classifiers**: deliberately scale *down* the output layer's weights at initialization (e.g. by a factor of 10). This produces smaller, closer-together logits at the very start of training, meaning the model starts out appropriately unconfident rather than accidentally very confident and very wrong — which avoids extreme initial losses and the resulting huge, destabilizing gradient updates.

## Better Activation Functions

Even with good initialization, the *choice* of activation function still matters enormously for how well gradients survive the trip through a deep network.

*   **Why ReLU became the default**: it doesn't saturate at all for positive inputs (no flattening, no vanishing gradient there), and it's extremely cheap to compute. Neither of these is true of sigmoid.
*   **The dying ReLU problem**: during training, a neuron can end up in a state where its weighted input is negative for *every* training instance it ever sees. Since ReLU's output (and gradient) is exactly 0 for any negative input, that neuron now outputs nothing but zeros forever, and gradient descent has no gradient to work with to revive it — it's permanently "dead." This can affect a large fraction of a network's neurons, especially when the learning rate is too high. (A dead neuron *can* occasionally come back to life if the layers feeding it change enough over training to push its input positive again — but this isn't guaranteed.)
*   **Leaky ReLU**: $\text{LeakyReLU}_\alpha(z) = \max(\alpha z, z)$ — instead of a completely flat 0 for negative inputs, it has a small nonzero slope $\alpha$ (the "leak"). This means a leaky ReLU neuron can never fully die — it can go dormant for a long stretch, but there's always at least a small gradient available to eventually revive it. Somewhat counterintuitively, research found a large leak ($\alpha=0.2$) tends to outperform a small one ($\alpha=0.01$). Variants: **RReLU** (randomized leaky ReLU — $\alpha$ is randomly sampled during training and fixed to an average at test time; this randomness acts as a mild regularizer, reducing overfitting) and **PReLU** (parametric leaky ReLU — $\alpha$ itself becomes a trainable parameter, learned via backprop just like any weight; strong on large datasets, but prone to overfitting on smaller ones since it's extra flexibility the model can misuse). PyTorch: `nn.LeakyReLU`, `nn.RReLU`, `nn.PReLU`. Because the negative slope changes the effective variance of the layer's output, Kaiming initialization needs a corresponding adjustment (scaled down by a factor of $1+\alpha^2$) — pass `nonlinearity="leaky_relu"` and the `alpha` value to `kaiming_uniform_()`/`kaiming_normal_()` to get this automatically.
*   **The smoothness problem with ReLU-family functions**: ReLU, Leaky ReLU, and PReLU all have an abrupt kink at $z=0$ — the slope changes instantaneously rather than smoothly. Just like the non-smooth kink in Lasso's cost function (Training Models chapter), this can make gradient descent bounce around near that point rather than converging cleanly.
*   **ELU** (exponential linear unit): $\text{ELU}_\alpha(z) = \alpha(e^z - 1)$ for $z<0$, else $z$. Differences from plain ReLU: it takes on small *negative* values for negative inputs (pulling the neuron's average output closer to 0, which — much like using tanh over sigmoid — helps counteract vanishing gradients); it has a genuinely nonzero gradient everywhere for $z<0$, avoiding the dying-neuron problem entirely; and, with $\alpha=1$, it's smooth everywhere including at $z=0$, avoiding the bouncing issue above. Its downside: computing an exponential is meaningfully slower than ReLU's simple `max(0, z)`, so ELU networks run a bit slower both to train and at inference, even though they often converge in fewer *epochs*. PyTorch: `nn.ELU`.
*   **SELU** (scaled ELU): roughly $1.05 \times \text{ELU}_{1.67}(z)$. Its notable property: an MLP built *entirely* from dense layers using SELU activation everywhere will **self-normalize** — each layer's output tends to naturally maintain a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1 as training proceeds, which sidesteps vanishing/exploding gradients without needing any explicit normalization layer. This only actually happens under fairly strict conditions, though: the input features must already be standardized; every hidden layer must use LeCun normal initialization; the architecture must be a *plain* stack of dense layers (no skip connections like Wide & Deep, no recurrent connections); and you cannot combine it with $\ell_1$/$\ell_2$ regularization, batch-norm, layer-norm, max-norm, or regular dropout (there's a SELU-compatible dropout variant — see below). Given how restrictive this list is, SELU never gained the traction its early promise suggested, and other activations tend to outperform it in practice anyway. PyTorch: `nn.SELU`.
*   **GELU** (Gaussian Error Linear Unit): $\text{GELU}(z) = z\Phi(z)$, where $\Phi$ is the cumulative distribution function of a standard normal distribution (i.e., $\Phi(z)$ is the probability that a value randomly drawn from that distribution falls below $z$). Visually it resembles ReLU (approaching 0 for very negative $z$, approaching $z$ for very positive $z$), but unlike every function discussed so far, it's neither *convex* (a straight line between any two points on the curve always sits above the curve) nor *monotonic* (always increasing or always decreasing) — it dips slightly negative around $z \approx -0.75$ before curving back up. This added curvature seems to give gradient descent more to work with on genuinely complex tasks, and GELU often outperforms the simpler functions above — at the cost of being noticeably more expensive to compute (it can be closely approximated as $z\sigma(1.702z)$, which is cheaper and works nearly as well). PyTorch: `nn.GELU`.
*   **SiLU / Swish**: $z\sigma(z)$ — this is exactly GELU's fast approximation with the constant fixed to 1 rather than 1.702, and it was independently rediscovered by an automated search for good activation functions (hence the separate name "Swish," which stuck). The **generalized Swish** adds a scale hyperparameter: $\text{Swish}_\beta(z) = z\sigma(\beta z)$ (GELU is approximately Swish with $\beta=1.702$). $\beta$ can be tuned like any hyperparameter, or made *trainable* (one shared value per model, or per layer, similar in spirit to PReLU's learnable $\alpha$). PyTorch: `nn.SiLU`.
*   **SwiGLU**: runs the input through the Swish function *and*, in parallel, through a separate linear layer, then multiplies the two results elementwise: $\text{SwiGLU}(z) = \text{Swish}_\beta(z_1) \otimes z_2$ (in practice implemented by doubling a preceding linear layer's output width, then splitting it into two halves $z_1, z_2$). This elementwise multiplication is a **gating mechanism** — it gives the network a way to learn when to suppress (multiply by ~0) or amplify a given feature, adding real expressive power beyond a plain activation function. SwiGLU is extremely common in modern Transformer architectures (a later chapter).
*   **Mish**: $\text{mish}(z) = z\tanh(\text{softplus}(z))$, where $\text{softplus}(z) = \log(1+e^z)$. Another smooth, nonconvex, nonmonotonic ReLU-family function, empirically edging out both Swish and GELU by a small margin in the paper that introduced it — its curve overlaps almost exactly with Swish for negative $z$ and almost exactly with GELU for positive $z$. PyTorch: `nn.Mish`.
*   **ReLU²**: literally ReLU squared, $\text{ReLU}^2(z) = (\max(0,z))^2$ — found by an automated architecture search rather than hand-designed. It keeps ReLU's simplicity, cheapness, and sparse output (many neurons output exactly 0), while adding a smooth (nonzero-second-derivative) gradient right at $z=0$. It often outperforms other options for sparse models, but training can be less stable — it's more sensitive to outliers and can still suffer from dying neurons. Compute it directly as `F.relu(z).square()`.
*   **Practical guidance**: ReLU remains a solid, fast default for most tasks, and hardware/library optimizations specifically target it. For genuinely complex tasks, Swish (optionally with a learnable $\beta$) is often a better default. Mish and SwiGLU can squeeze out a bit more accuracy at extra compute cost. If runtime latency is the priority, favor Leaky ReLU, parametrized Leaky ReLU, or ReLU² (especially for sparse models). PyTorch also ships fast, simplified "Hard" approximations of several of these (`nn.Hardsigmoid`, `nn.Hardtanh`, `nn.Hardswish`), commonly used on mobile/embedded devices where every bit of compute counts.

## Batch Normalization

Good initialization and a good activation function reduce the *risk* of unstable gradients at the *start* of training — but they don't guarantee the problem won't resurface as training proceeds. **Batch normalization (BN)**, introduced by Ioffe and Szegedy in 2015, directly addresses this by normalizing the signal flowing *through* the network at every step of training, not just at initialization.

*   **Mechanism**: insert a BN operation just before or after a hidden layer's activation function. It (1) zero-centers and normalizes its input using statistics computed from the current mini-batch, then (2) rescales and shifts the result using two new *learned* parameter vectors per layer — one for scale, one for shift — letting the model learn whatever mean and spread actually works best for that layer's inputs, rather than being forced to accept plain standardization. If you put a BN layer as the very first layer of a network, it does this standardization job for you approximately, so you often don't need a separate `StandardScaler` step at all.
*   **The algorithm, step by step** (Equation 11-4): for a mini-batch $B$ with $m_B$ instances, compute the batch mean $\mu_B$ and variance $\sigma_B^2$ across the batch; normalize each instance's input as $\hat{x}^{(i)} = \frac{x^{(i)} - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \varepsilon}}$ (where $\varepsilon$, typically $10^{-5}$, is a tiny **smoothing term** preventing division by zero and keeping gradients from blowing up); then apply the learned scale and shift: $z^{(i)} = \gamma \otimes \hat{x}^{(i)} + \beta$.
*   **The test-time problem**: at prediction time you might be scoring one instance at a time (no batch to compute statistics from at all), or a batch too small/unrepresentative to trust. The standard fix is to track a running estimate of each BN layer's "final" mean and variance *during training* using an **exponential moving average** of the batch statistics seen so far, and to swap in these running estimates in place of the current batch's statistics once the model is switched to evaluation mode.
*   **Four learned/estimated quantities per BN layer**: $\gamma$ (scale) and $\beta$ (shift) are genuine trainable *parameters*, updated through ordinary backpropagation. The running mean and running variance are not trained by gradient descent at all — they're *estimated* via the moving average described above, and stored as **buffers** (regular tensors tracked alongside the model, but not gradient-tracked parameters). This is precisely why switching correctly between `model.train()` (use current-batch statistics) and `model.eval()` (use the accumulated running statistics) is critical — using the wrong one silently produces wrong or inconsistent predictions, without necessarily throwing any error.
*   **Real-world impact**: in the original paper's ImageNet experiments, batch norm reached the same accuracy in **14× fewer training steps**, tolerated much larger learning rates, drastically reduced the vanishing gradients problem (well enough that even saturating functions like tanh and sigmoid became usable again), made the network far less sensitive to weight initialization choices, and — as a nice side effect — acts as a decent regularizer in its own right, somewhat reducing the need for other regularization techniques like dropout.
*   **The cost**: extra model complexity, and a genuine runtime penalty at inference time from the additional per-layer computation. This penalty can often be eliminated after training by **fusing** the BN layer's math directly into the *previous* layer's weights and bias — since BN's operation on a preceding linear layer's output ($\mathbf{XW}+\mathbf{b}$) works out to another linear transformation once you substitute in the algebra, you can precompute an equivalent $\mathbf{W}'$ and $\mathbf{b}'$ and drop the BN layer entirely for inference. PyTorch's `optimize_for_inference()` (from the PyTorch chapter) performs exactly this fusion automatically.
*   Training with BN does tend to make *each epoch* slower (extra computation per layer), but since convergence generally needs far fewer epochs overall, total wall-clock training time is usually still shorter, not longer.
*   **PyTorch usage**: add an `nn.BatchNorm1d(num_features)` layer before or after each hidden layer's activation, specifying that layer's width. Its learnable parameters show up under `.named_parameters()` as `weight` (that's $\gamma$) and `bias` (that's $\beta$); its running statistics show up separately under `.named_buffers()` as `running_mean`, `running_var`, and `num_batches_tracked`.
*   **Before or after the activation function?** The original paper argues for placing BN *before* the activation. This is genuinely debated and seems to depend on the specific task, so it's worth experimenting with both. If you do place BN before the activation, you can safely drop the bias term from the preceding `nn.Linear` layer (`bias=False`) — the BN layer's own $\beta$ term already serves as a bias, so the two would otherwise be redundant. You can also skip the very first BN layer (avoiding "sandwiching" the first hidden layer between two BN layers back to back), but only if you standardize the training inputs yourself in that case.
*   **A genuinely easy-to-miss PyTorch quirk**: `nn.BatchNorm1d`'s `momentum` hyperparameter (used in updating the exponential moving average) has the *opposite* meaning from what "momentum" conventionally means elsewhere (including in PyTorch's own optimizers) — here, a value close to **0** means slow-changing/heavily-smoothed running statistics ("more friction"), while a value close to 1 means the running average snaps quickly to each new batch. A good value is typically small — 0.01 or 0.001 — and smaller mini-batches generally want an even smaller value than the 0.1 default (which is tuned more for large batch sizes).
*   **1D vs. 2D vs. 3D batch norm**: `nn.BatchNorm1d` expects `[batch_size, num_features]` inputs — the same shape `nn.Linear` expects — so images must be flattened first if you're using it that way. `nn.BatchNorm2d` instead expects raw `[batch_size, channels, height, width]` image batches and computes statistics per *channel* across both the batch and the spatial dimensions (so a 3-channel RGB image gets exactly 3 scale/shift parameters, one per color channel) — this generally works better for image data than flattening first. `nn.BatchNorm3d` handles volumetric data (`[batch, channels, depth, height, width]`, e.g. CT scans). `nn.BatchNorm1d` can also normalize *sequence* data (shape `[batch, sequence_length, num_features]` by PyTorch convention) — but it actually expects `[batch, num_features, sequence_length]`, so you must permute the last two dimensions (`X.permute(0, 2, 1)`) before feeding sequence data through it (sequences are covered further in a later chapter).
*   BN has become so standard in deep convolutional networks (next chapter) that architecture diagrams often just assume it's present after every layer without drawing it explicitly. It's not flawless, though — its batch-dependent statistics can hurt performance with small batch sizes, and it interacts poorly with recurrent architectures — which is why **layer normalization** has increasingly replaced it in many modern architectures.

## Layer Normalization

*   **Layer normalization (LN)** is structurally similar to batch norm, but normalizes across the **feature dimensions** of a single instance instead of across the batch dimension. Introduced in 2016 initially for recurrent networks, it's since spread to convolutional nets, Transformers, and diffusion models.
*   Because it only ever needs the current instance's own feature values (not a whole batch), LN computes its statistics on the fly, independently per instance, at both training and evaluation time — it **behaves identically in both modes**, with no need for the moving-average machinery batch norm requires. It still learns a per-feature scale and offset, just like BN.
*   **PyTorch usage**: `nn.LayerNorm(normalized_shape)`, where `normalized_shape` specifies the trailing dimension(s) to normalize over. For example, given a batch of RGB images shaped `[batch, 3, height, width]`, `nn.LayerNorm([height, width])` normalizes each color channel of each image independently, while `nn.LayerNorm([3, height, width])` (the more common choice in vision architectures) normalizes each image across all three channels at once.

## Gradient Clipping

*   A more direct fix specifically for **exploding** gradients: cap ("clip") gradients during backpropagation so they can never exceed some threshold. Most useful where batch-norm-style solutions are awkward to apply — commonly recurrent neural networks (a later chapter).
*   **`torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(params, max_norm)`** rescales each gradient *vector* down if its overall norm (magnitude) exceeds `max_norm`, which preserves the gradient's *direction* — it just shrinks its length.
*   **`torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_value_(params, clip_value)`** instead clips each individual gradient *component* independently to the range $[-\text{clip\_value}, +\text{clip\_value}]$. This can visibly change the gradient vector's *direction*, not just its length — e.g. a gradient of $[0.9, 100.0]$ (pointing almost entirely along the second axis) clips-by-value to $[0.9, 1.0]$ (pointing roughly diagonally), whereas clipping-by-norm on the same vector would instead produce $[0.009, 1.0]$ (preserving direction, but nearly zeroing the first component). Despite the reorientation, clipping by value works well in practice too — which one is better is dataset-dependent.
*   Call whichever clipping function right after `loss.backward()` and before `optimizer.step()`.

## Reusing Pretrained Layers (Transfer Learning)

*   Training a very large network completely from scratch is usually the wrong first move — it's almost always worth checking first for an existing network trained on a similar task, and reusing most of its layers (typically all but the last one or few). This is **transfer learning**, and done well it speeds up training substantially *and* needs far less labeled data for the new task.
*   **How many layers to reuse**: the output layer of the original model is essentially never directly useful (it's tailored to the original task's specific classes/outputs), so it gets replaced. Beyond that, lower layers tend to have learned more general, broadly-reusable features, while upper layers have learned more task-specific ones — so the more similar the new task is to the original, the *more* layers (especially lower ones) are worth keeping.
*   **A staged strategy**: freeze all reused layers first (`requires_grad = False` on their parameters, so gradient descent can't touch them) and train just the new layer(s) for a while, letting them settle into reasonable values before backprop is allowed to disturb the reused weights. Then try unfreezing the top one or two reused layers and continuing training, checking whether performance improves — the more training data available, the more layers it's usually safe to unfreeze. When unfreezing, drop the learning rate, since a high rate applied to already-well-tuned reused weights can wreck them quickly. If none of this gets good performance and you have little data, try instead *dropping* the top hidden layer(s) entirely and re-freezing what's left, iterating on how many layers to keep. With abundant data for the new task, you can instead try replacing (or even adding) top hidden layers rather than dropping them.
*   Inputs need to match what the reused network expects — resize images to the original's expected input size if needed — and transfer learning generally only helps when the new task's inputs share *low-level* structure with the original (a network trained on regular phone photos will transfer reasonably to other phone-photo tasks, but probably won't help at all with satellite or medical imagery).
*   **PyTorch mechanics**: use `copy.deepcopy()` to duplicate the reused portion of an existing `nn.Sequential` model (e.g. `model_A[:-1]` to grab everything but the final layer), then build a new `nn.Sequential` from those copied layers plus a freshly-initialized output layer sized for the new task. Freeze the copied layers with a loop setting `param.requires_grad = False` on each of their parameters. Remember to switch loss function and metric to match the new task (e.g. `nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss` and a `task="binary"` accuracy metric for a binary classification target, as covered in the PyTorch chapter) — the loss/metric belong to the *new* task, not the old one.
*   **An important, deliberately honest caveat straight from the book**: the chapter demonstrates transfer learning taking a tiny 20-image dataset from 71.6% to 92.5% test accuracy — and then explicitly admits this result was *cherry-picked*: many configurations and random seeds were tried until one produced a strong-looking improvement, and the effect shrinks, vanishes, or even reverses under different seeds or class choices. This is called **"torturing the data until it confesses"**, a specific case of **p-hacking** — quietly trying many variants and reporting only the flattering result, without disclosing the failures along the way. It's one of the reasons many published results in science don't reproduce, and it's worth internalizing as a general skepticism reflex: a result that looks unusually clean deserves suspicion, not automatic trust. **The underlying, non-cherry-picked truth**: transfer learning tends *not* to work particularly well for small, plain dense (fully-connected) networks — small networks simply haven't learned many generalizable patterns, and dense layers tend to learn narrow, task-specific patterns that don't transfer cleanly to a different task. Transfer learning works far better with deep convolutional networks and Transformer architectures (later chapters), where it genuinely delivers the gains this simple MLP example was staged to illustrate.

## Unsupervised Pretraining

*   If no similar pretrained model exists *and* you don't have much labeled data — but you *can* get your hands on a lot of cheap, **unlabeled** data for the same domain — you can pretrain in an unsupervised way. Train an unsupervised model (an **autoencoder**, covered in a later chapter, being the modern go-to) on the unlabeled data, then reuse its lower layers, attach a task-specific output layer, and fine-tune the whole thing with your (comparatively small) labeled dataset.
*   This is historically significant: it's the exact technique Geoffrey Hinton's team used in 2006 to revive interest in deep learning after it had fallen out of favor. Until around 2010, unsupervised pretraining (then typically using **restricted Boltzmann machines**, RBMs) was standard practice for training deep nets at all — it only became less necessary once the vanishing gradients problem was substantially alleviated by the techniques earlier in this chapter, at which point purely supervised training of deep nets from scratch became routine.
*   **Historical technique**: **greedy layer-wise pretraining** — train a single-layer unsupervised model (e.g. an RBM), freeze it, stack a new layer on top and train just that new layer, freeze it too, stack another, and so on, building the network up one layer at a time. Modern practice has simplified this considerably: train the *entire* unsupervised model (typically an autoencoder or a diffusion model rather than an RBM) in one shot on all the unlabeled data at once, rather than layer by layer.
*   Unsupervised pretraining remains a legitimately good option today specifically when: the task is complex, there's no similar model to reuse via ordinary transfer learning, labeled data is scarce, but unlabeled data is abundant.

## Pretraining on an Auxiliary Task

*   A related last-resort option when labeled data is scarce: train a first network on a different, **auxiliary task** for which labeled (or automatically-labelable) data is cheap or easy to obtain, then reuse that network's lower layers — which will have learned generally-useful feature detectors — for your actual target task.
*   **Face recognition example**: with only a handful of photos per person (nowhere near enough to train a classifier directly), you could instead train a first network on a large public dataset of faces (e.g. VGGFace2) to solve the *auxiliary* task "do these two photos show the same person?" — a task with effectively unlimited labeled pairs available. That network's lower layers will have learned generally useful facial feature detectors, which you can then reuse to build a good classifier for your actual, data-scarce task.
*   Worth noting explicitly: scraping random people's photos off the web to build such a dataset yourself would likely be illegal — photos are typically copyrighted by their creators (and platforms like Instagram/Facebook contractually prohibit scraping), and dozens of countries legally require explicit consent to collect and process personal data including facial images. Use properly licensed/consented datasets.
*   **NLP example**: download a large corpus of ordinary text, then automatically generate a labeled training task from it with no manual labeling at all — e.g. randomly mask out words and train a model to predict the missing word (given "What ___ you saying?", the model should learn to predict "are" or "were"). A model trained well on this task ends up having learned a great deal about language generally, and can be reused/fine-tuned for your actual task. This is essentially how large language models are pretrained and then fine-tuned (a later chapter goes into this in depth).
*   **Self-supervised learning** is the general name for this pattern: automatically deriving labels from the data itself (as in the masking example above), then training with ordinary supervised-learning techniques on the resulting "labeled" dataset — no human labeling required at all.

## Faster Optimizers

Good initialization, a good activation function, normalization layers, and reusing pretrained weights all speed up training. A fifth, independent lever: swapping plain gradient descent for a smarter **optimizer**.

*   **Momentum optimization** (Polyak, 1964): plain gradient descent takes a step purely proportional to the *current* local gradient — small steps on a gentle slope, big steps on a steep one — and never builds up speed, the way a bowling ball rolling down a smooth slope does. Momentum optimization fixes this by tracking a **momentum vector** $\mathbf{m}$ that accumulates gradients over time, and updating the weights by *adding* that momentum vector rather than subtracting the raw gradient directly:
    $$\mathbf{m} \leftarrow \beta\mathbf{m} - \eta\nabla_\theta J(\theta), \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta + \mathbf{m}$$
    The **momentum coefficient** $\beta$ (typically 0.9) acts like friction, keeping the momentum from growing without bound — with $\beta=0.9$, the eventual "terminal velocity" of the updates works out to about **10× faster** than plain gradient descent under a constant gradient. In practice the speedup isn't usually that dramatic (real gradients aren't constant), but momentum genuinely helps escape plateaus and roll through the "elongated bowl" cost surfaces that arise when input features have very different scales (see the Training Models chapter) — a common situation for the upper layers of a deep net that isn't using batch/layer norm. A side effect of the accumulated momentum: the optimizer can overshoot the minimum, swing back, overshoot again, and oscillate a few times before settling — which is exactly why a bit of "friction" (β below 1) helps convergence rather than hurting it. PyTorch: `torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), momentum=0.9, lr=...)`.
*   **Nesterov accelerated gradient (NAG)** (Nesterov, 1983): a small but consistently helpful tweak to momentum — instead of measuring the gradient at the *current* position $\theta$, measure it slightly *ahead*, at $\theta + \beta\mathbf{m}$ (i.e., where the momentum is already about to carry you):
    $$\mathbf{m} \leftarrow \beta\mathbf{m} - \eta\nabla_\theta J(\theta + \beta\mathbf{m}), \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta + \mathbf{m}$$
    Since the momentum vector is usually already pointing roughly toward the optimum, this "look-ahead" gradient tends to be a slightly more accurate correction than the gradient measured at the starting point — and, notably, when momentum is carrying the weights *across* a valley, NAG's look-ahead gradient pushes back *toward* the bottom of the valley rather than continuing to push further across it, damping the oscillation problem described above. PyTorch: `torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), momentum=0.9, nesterov=True, lr=...)`.
*   **AdaGrad**: targets the same "elongated bowl" problem from a different angle — rather than building up speed, it *scales down* the learning rate more aggressively along whichever dimensions have historically had the steepest gradients, correcting the direction of travel earlier so it points more directly at the optimum:
    $$\mathbf{s} \leftarrow \mathbf{s} + \nabla_\theta J(\theta) \otimes \nabla_\theta J(\theta), \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \oslash \sqrt{\mathbf{s}+\varepsilon}$$
    ($\mathbf{s}$ accumulates the sum of squared gradients seen so far, per parameter — a steep dimension accumulates a larger $s_i$ and gets its effective learning rate shrunk more.) This is called an **adaptive learning rate**, and it needs much less manual learning-rate tuning as a nice side benefit. Its fatal flaw for neural networks: the accumulation runs over the *entire* training history and only ever grows, so the effective learning rate keeps shrinking and shrinking until training grinds to a halt — often well before actually reaching a good solution. **Don't use AdaGrad to train deep neural networks** (it's fine for simpler, genuinely convex problems like plain linear regression); it's covered here mainly because understanding it is the easiest path to understanding RMSProp and Adam below.
*   **RMSProp**: fixes AdaGrad's premature-stopping flaw by only accumulating gradients from the *recent past*, via **exponential decay**, rather than the entire training history:
    $$\mathbf{s} \leftarrow \alpha\mathbf{s} + (1-\alpha)\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\otimes\nabla_\theta J(\theta), \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\oslash\sqrt{\mathbf{s}+\varepsilon}$$
    The decay rate $\alpha$ (typically 0.9) rarely needs tuning. RMSProp reliably outperforms AdaGrad on real neural network training, and was the go-to optimizer for many practitioners before Adam arrived. PyTorch: `torch.optim.RMSprop(model.parameters(), alpha=0.9, lr=...)`.
*   **Adam** ("adaptive moment estimation"): combines momentum's idea (tracking a decaying average of past *gradients*, called the **first moment**) with RMSProp's idea (tracking a decaying average of past *squared* gradients — the **second moment**), plus a bias-correction step (steps 3–4 below) needed because both moving averages start at zero and would otherwise be artificially biased toward zero early in training:
    $$\mathbf{m} \leftarrow \beta_1\mathbf{m} - (1-\beta_1)\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$$
    $$\mathbf{s} \leftarrow \beta_2\mathbf{s} + (1-\beta_2)\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\otimes\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$$
    $$\hat{\mathbf{m}} \leftarrow \frac{\mathbf{m}}{1-\beta_1^{\,t}}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{s}} \leftarrow \frac{\mathbf{s}}{1-\beta_2^{\,t}}$$
    $$\theta \leftarrow \theta + \eta\hat{\mathbf{m}} \oslash \sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{s}}+\varepsilon}$$
    Default hyperparameters ($\beta_1{=}0.9$, $\beta_2{=}0.999$, $\varepsilon{=}10^{-8}$) work well nearly out of the box, and — being adaptive like AdaGrad/RMSProp — Adam needs much less learning-rate tuning than plain gradient descent (the default $\eta=0.001$ is a very reasonable starting point). PyTorch: `torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), betas=(0.9, 0.999), lr=...)`.
*   **AdaMax**: an Adam variant that replaces the $\ell_2$-norm-based scaling in step 2 with an $\ell_\infty$-norm (max) based scaling instead. Can be more numerically stable than Adam on some tasks, but Adam generally performs better overall — worth trying only if you're specifically running into problems with plain Adam.
*   **NAdam**: Adam plus the Nesterov look-ahead trick, generally converging a bit faster than Adam — though it's occasionally outperformed by plain RMSProp, so it's not a strict universal improvement.
*   **AdamW**: integrates **weight decay** (shrinking every weight slightly toward zero at each update, e.g. multiplying by 0.99) properly into Adam. Weight decay looks similar to $\ell_2$ regularization, and for plain SGD the two are mathematically *equivalent* — but for Adam and its variants, they are **not equivalent**, and naively combining plain Adam with $\ell_2$ regularization tends to produce models that generalize noticeably worse than the SGD-with-weight-decay equivalent. AdamW fixes this mismatch by decoupling weight decay from the adaptive gradient scaling. PyTorch: swap `torch.optim.Adam` for `torch.optim.NAdam`, `torch.optim.Adamax`, or `torch.optim.AdamW` respectively; for AdamW you'll want to tune the `weight_decay` hyperparameter specifically.
*   **A caution worth remembering**: research (Wilson et al., 2017) has shown that adaptive methods (RMSProp, Adam and its variants) can sometimes converge to solutions that generalize *worse* than plain SGD-with-momentum on certain datasets, despite converging faster during training. If a model's performance is disappointingly weak, it's worth trying NAG as an alternative before assuming the architecture itself is the problem.
*   **Second-order (Hessian-based) methods** exist in the broader optimization literature and use curvature information (the *second*-order partial derivatives, not just the gradient/first-order derivatives used by everything above), but are generally impractical for deep networks directly — the Hessian has $n^2$ entries for $n$ parameters, which is far too much memory and compute for networks with hundreds of thousands of parameters or more. Stochastic approximations like **Shampoo** make a second-order-inspired approach practical (available via the separate `torch_optimizer` package, not PyTorch core).
*   **Training sparse models** (most weights exactly zero, for speed/memory at inference): the optimizers above all naturally produce *dense* models. To get sparsity instead, either train normally and then zero out small weights afterward (`torch.nn.utils.prune.l1_unstructured()` for individual weights, `prune.ln_structured()` to drop entire neurons/channels/layers), and/or apply strong $\ell_1$ regularization during training (which specifically pushes the optimizer toward exact zeros — see below), and/or scale down the random initial weights to encourage sparsity from the start.
*   **Rough comparison** (convergence speed and typical solution quality — take as general tendencies, not guarantees): plain SGD is slow but reliable; momentum and Nesterov-momentum SGD converge noticeably faster while usually reaching comparably good solutions; AdaGrad converges reasonably fast but tends to stop too early on neural nets (poor final quality); RMSProp, Adam, AdaMax, NAdam, and AdamW all converge fast, with final solution quality that's typically good-to-great but can occasionally lag behind well-tuned SGD-with-momentum on certain datasets (per the caution above).

## Learning Rate Scheduling

A fixed learning rate is rarely ideal: too high and training diverges; too low and it's painfully slow (and can get permanently stuck); a rate that's high-but-not-diverging tends to make fast early progress but then dance around the optimum indefinitely instead of settling into it. The fix, in general, is to start with a fairly high learning rate for the fast early progress, and reduce it over the course of training so the model can settle precisely near the end — a strategy called a **learning schedule**, implemented in PyTorch via the `torch.optim.lr_scheduler` module.

*   **Exponential scheduling** (`ExponentialLR`): multiplies the learning rate by a fixed factor `gamma` (< 1, but close to 1) at the end of every epoch, so after $n$ epochs the rate is $\eta_0 \cdot \gamma^n$. Requires calling `scheduler.step()` once per epoch (after the batch loop). If resuming interrupted training, set the scheduler's `last_epoch` argument to wherever you left off (default `-1` restarts the schedule from scratch).
*   **Cosine annealing** (`CosineAnnealingLR`): decreases the learning rate along a cosine curve from a maximum $\eta_{max}$ down to a minimum $\eta_{min}$ over `T_max` epochs:
    $$\eta_t = \eta_{min} + \frac{1}{2}(\eta_{max}-\eta_{min})\left(1+\cos\left(\frac{t}{T_{max}}\pi\right)\right)$$
    Compared to exponential decay, this keeps the learning rate fairly high for most of training and only drops it sharply near the end, which tends to perform better in practice — its downside is having to guess `T_max` and `eta_min` in advance, without knowing ahead of time how long training will actually need.
*   **Performance scheduling** (`ReduceLROnPlateau`, also called *adaptive scheduling*): tracks a chosen metric (commonly validation loss or accuracy) and multiplies the learning rate by a `factor` whenever that metric stops improving for `patience` consecutive epochs. Key settings: `mode="max"` if the tracked metric should be maximized (e.g. accuracy — the default `"min"` is for something like loss); `patience` (default 10); `factor` (default 0.1). Because it needs the current metric value, you must evaluate it each epoch and pass it explicitly: `scheduler.step(val_metric)`.
*   **Learning rate warmup**: rather than *always* starting at the maximum rate, sometimes it helps to start near zero and ramp linearly up to the target rate over the first few epochs. This matters most for sensitive architectures (recurrent networks) or very large batch sizes, where starting at full speed can cause gradient descent to bounce around randomly without making progress. The intuition: a spiky, mountainous loss landscape is dangerous to cross at high speed early on (you can bounce from peak to peak indefinitely) — a small initial learning rate lets training first find its way down into flatter terrain, after which a high learning rate becomes safe and productive. Implement with `LinearLR` (ramps linearly between a `start_factor` and `end_factor` of the base rate over `total_iters` epochs) or a fully custom `LambdaLR`. Call the warmup scheduler's `.step()` at the *start* of each epoch, and make sure any other scheduler you're also using stays inactive until the warmup phase finishes.
*   **Cosine annealing with warm restarts** (`CosineAnnealingWarmRestarts`): repeats the cosine annealing cycle over and over, with the learning rate periodically shooting back up to its maximum — this deliberately gives gradient descent repeated chances to escape a local optimum or a stubborn plateau it might otherwise be stuck on. The recommended pattern is to start with a short cycle (`T_0`) and *double* its length every restart (`T_mult=2`), giving lots of quick exploration early on and progressively longer, more careful optimization phases as training continues.
*   **1cycle scheduling** (`OneCycleLR`): warms the learning rate up linearly from $\eta_0$ to a maximum $\eta_1$ over the first half of training, then decreases it linearly back down to $\eta_0$ over the second half, finishing the last few epochs by dropping it by several more orders of magnitude. When used with a momentum-based optimizer, momentum is scheduled in the *opposite* direction — starting high (e.g. 0.95), dropping to a lower value (e.g. 0.85) during the learning-rate warmup phase, then climbing back to the high value as the learning rate cools down. In the paper introducing it, this approach reached 91.9% validation accuracy on CIFAR-10 in just 100 epochs, versus 90.3% in 800 epochs with a standard fixed schedule on the same architecture — dubbed **super-convergence**.
*   **Practical recommendation**: 1cycle is a solid default if you're unsure where to start; performance scheduling is a common alternative many practitioners lean on; add warmup specifically if you see instability right at the start of training; reach for cosine annealing with warm restarts specifically if training is getting stuck on plateaus.

## Avoiding Overfitting Through Regularization

Deep networks routinely have tens of thousands to billions of parameters — an enormous amount of freedom to fit a training set, and correspondingly high risk of overfitting it rather than genuinely generalizing. Early stopping (Training Models chapter) is already one regularizer in the toolkit, and batch-norm/layer-norm both act as mild regularizers as a side effect of what they're designed to do. Beyond those:

*   **$\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ regularization**: exactly as in linear models (Training Models chapter), $\ell_2$ regularization constrains weights to stay small, while $\ell_1$ regularization pushes toward genuinely *sparse* weights (many exactly zero). For **plain SGD** (with or without momentum), $\ell_2$ regularization is mathematically identical to **weight decay**, so it's implementable directly via the optimizer's `weight_decay` argument: `torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=..., weight_decay=1e-4)`. For **Adam**, switch to **AdamW** and set `weight_decay` there instead (as noted above, this isn't exactly equivalent to $\ell_2$ regularization, but is close and tends to work better than the naive combination). Weight decay by default applies to *every* parameter, including bias terms and batch-norm/layer-norm parameters — regularizing those rarely helps and can occasionally hurt. To exclude them, either compute the $\ell_2$ (or $\ell_1$, which PyTorch has no built-in helper for at all) loss term manually over just the parameters you want and add it to the main loss, or use PyTorch's **parameter groups** feature: pass the optimizer a list of dictionaries, each specifying a `"params"` list plus group-specific hyperparameter overrides (e.g. one group with `weight_decay` for the regularized weights, a second group with no `weight_decay` for biases/norm parameters). Parameter groups are also the standard mechanism for applying different learning rates to different parts of a model — most commonly in transfer learning, where you want newly-added layers to learn faster than the reused ones.
*   **Dropout**: at every training step, every neuron in a layer using dropout (including input neurons, but *never* output neurons) has an independent probability $p$ (the **dropout rate**, typically 10–50%; closer to 20–30% for recurrent nets, 40–50% for convolutional nets) of being entirely zeroed out for that step — ignored on the forward pass, contributing nothing, but possibly active again on the next step. After training finishes, dropout is switched off entirely, and every neuron participates normally. Despite sounding purely destructive, it reliably delivers real gains (often a 1–2 percentage-point accuracy boost, which is a substantial relative drop in error rate for an already-strong model). **Why it works**: a neuron trained under dropout can't lean on any particular set of neighboring neurons always being present (they might vanish any given step), so it's forced to become independently useful and to draw on a broad range of its inputs rather than over-relying on just a few — producing a network that's more robust and less sensitive to small input changes (the book's analogy: a company where every employee might randomly be absent any given day is forced to spread expertise around rather than concentrating it in one irreplaceable person). Another lens on why it works: because each of the $2^N$ possible present/absent neuron combinations produces a functionally different network, training under dropout is effectively training an enormous ensemble of different (though weight-sharing) networks simultaneously, and the trained result behaves like an average over that whole ensemble. Dropout tends to help the *upper* hidden layers most (they're the ones learning the most complex, most overfit-prone feature combinations), so it's common to apply it only to the last one to three hidden layers rather than every layer. **A technical detail that matters**: if a fraction $p$ of neurons are dropped on average during training, each neuron effectively receives roughly $\frac{1}{1-p}$ times more total input once dropout is switched off at test time — a mismatch large enough to break the model if left uncorrected. The standard fix, handled automatically by `nn.Dropout`, is to scale the surviving inputs up by dividing by the **keep probability** $(1-p)$ during training, so no adjustment is needed at test time. **PyTorch usage**: insert `nn.Dropout(p=...)` before the layers you want it to affect, and — exactly as with batch norm — it's essential to correctly switch `model.train()` (dropout active) vs. `model.eval()` (dropout fully disabled, passthrough). Because dropout is only active during training, comparing training loss to validation loss directly can be misleading (a model can be overfitting even while the two losses look similar, since the training loss is measured under a handicapped, dropout-degraded version of the model) — evaluate the actual training loss without dropout active if you want a fair comparison. Tuning direction: increase the dropout rate if the model is overfitting, decrease it if it's underfitting; larger layers can generally tolerate a higher rate than small ones. Dropout does slow down convergence noticeably, but is usually well worth the extra training time for the resulting improvement, especially on large models. **Alpha dropout** (`nn.AlphaDropout`) is a SELU-compatible variant that preserves the mean and standard deviation of its inputs (rather than zeroing values outright) — needed specifically because regular dropout would break SELU's self-normalizing property.
*   **Monte Carlo (MC) dropout** (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016): a technique that boosts an *already-trained* dropout model's performance with zero retraining and only a few lines of extra code — and gives dropout a rigorous mathematical grounding (a formal connection to approximate Bayesian inference) along the way. The trick: keep dropout **active during evaluation** too (rather than switching it off), so each forward pass through the model is slightly random. Instead of making one prediction, run the *same* input through the model many times (each time with a different random dropout pattern), convert each run's logits to probabilities via softmax, and **average the resulting probabilities** (not the raw logits — averaging logits first and applying softmax only at the end produces systematically overconfident results, since it skips the actual randomness-driven disagreement between runs). This tends to produce both more accurate predictions and — crucially — genuinely useful **uncertainty estimates**: the *standard deviation* across the repeated runs for a given class tells you how much the model's confidence in that prediction actually varies, which a single ordinary forward pass can never reveal (a model reporting 99% confidence with dropout's runs agreeing tightly is a very different situation from 99% confidence with runs disagreeing wildly, even though a single plain forward pass looks identical in both cases). This matters most for risk-sensitive applications (e.g. you'd rather a self-driving car know when it's *unsure* about a stop sign than confidently guess wrong). Implementation sketch: call `model.eval()` as usual, then loop over the model's submodules and call `.train()` specifically on any `nn.Dropout` layers found, so only dropout re-activates; repeat each input several times (e.g. via `.repeat_interleave()`), run them all through the model in one batch, reshape to separate the repeats back out per original instance, softmax, then average over the repeat dimension. The number of repeated forward passes (Monte Carlo samples) is a latency/accuracy trade-off hyperparameter — more samples give more accurate probability *and* uncertainty estimates, with diminishing returns past a certain point. If training a model specifically to use MC dropout from the start (rather than repurposing an already-trained one), a small custom module that always runs dropout in training mode regardless of the model's overall mode is more robust than manually toggling `nn.Dropout` submodules after the fact.
*   **Max-norm regularization**: for each neuron, constrains the $\ell_2$ norm of its *incoming* weight vector to never exceed a chosen threshold $r$ (the max-norm hyperparameter): $\|\mathbf{w}\|_2 \le r$. Smaller $r$ means stronger regularization. Unlike $\ell_1$/$\ell_2$ regularization, this isn't implemented as an extra term added to the loss function — instead, after every optimizer step, you directly measure each neuron's current weight norm and rescale it back down if it's exceeded $r$ (`param *= target_norm / (epsilon + actual_norm)`, applied per-neuron). It can also help alleviate unstable gradients as a side effect, specifically in networks *not* already using batch-norm or layer-norm. When applying it to layer types other than a plain `nn.Linear` (e.g. convolutional layers, in a later chapter), the exact dimension(s) to compute the norm over needs adjusting accordingly.

## Practical Guidelines

A reasonable, low-tuning-effort **default configuration** for a deep network: He (Kaiming) initialization; ReLU for shallow networks, Swish for deep ones; no normalization layer for shallow networks, batch-norm or layer-norm for deep ones; early stopping (plus weight decay if further regularization is needed); NAG or AdamW as the optimizer; performance scheduling or 1cycle for the learning rate schedule. On top of this, actively look for a pretrained network to reuse (transfer learning) if one exists for a similar task, or fall back to unsupervised pretraining (plenty of unlabeled data available) or auxiliary-task pretraining (plenty of labeled data for a *related* task) if not.

Exceptions worth deviating from the defaults for:

*   **Need a sparse model** → use $\ell_1$ regularization, and/or zero out small weights after training (`torch.nn.utils.prune.l1_unstructured()`). Note this breaks SELU's self-normalization if you were relying on it, so fall back to the default configuration in that case.
*   **Need low inference latency** → use fewer layers; a fast activation function (ReLU, Leaky ReLU, or Hardswish); fold batch-norm/layer-norm layers into the preceding layer after training (as described above); favor a sparse model; and consider reducing numeric precision from 32-bit floats down to 16-bit or even 8-bit (covered further as a general model-optimization technique).
*   **Risk-sensitive application, or latency isn't a major concern** → use MC dropout to get both a performance boost and genuinely useful uncertainty estimates alongside predictions.

## Formulas

**Glorot (Xavier) initialization:**
$$\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{fan_{avg}}, \qquad \text{or uniform in } [-r, r] \text{ with } r = \sqrt{\frac{3}{fan_{avg}}}$$

**He (Kaiming) initialization (ReLU family):** $\sigma^2 = \dfrac{2}{fan_{in}}$    **LeCun initialization (SELU):** $\sigma^2 = \dfrac{1}{fan_{in}}$

**Leaky ReLU:** $\text{LeakyReLU}_\alpha(z) = \max(\alpha z, z)$

**ELU:** $\text{ELU}_\alpha(z) = \begin{cases}\alpha(e^z-1) & z<0 \\ z & z \ge 0\end{cases}$

**GELU:** $\text{GELU}(z) = z\Phi(z) \approx z\sigma(1.702z)$    **Swish:** $\text{Swish}_\beta(z) = z\sigma(\beta z)$    **Mish:** $\text{mish}(z) = z\tanh(\text{softplus}(z))$    **ReLU²:** $\text{ReLU}^2(z) = (\max(0,z))^2$

**Batch normalization:**
$$\mu_B = \frac{1}{m_B}\sum_{i=1}^{m_B} x^{(i)}, \qquad \sigma_B^2 = \frac{1}{m_B}\sum_{i=1}^{m_B} (x^{(i)}-\mu_B)^2$$
$$\hat{x}^{(i)} = \frac{x^{(i)}-\mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2+\varepsilon}}, \qquad z^{(i)} = \gamma \otimes \hat{x}^{(i)} + \beta$$

**Momentum:** $\mathbf{m} \leftarrow \beta\mathbf{m} - \eta\nabla_\theta J(\theta), \quad \theta \leftarrow \theta + \mathbf{m}$

**Nesterov accelerated gradient:** $\mathbf{m} \leftarrow \beta\mathbf{m} - \eta\nabla_\theta J(\theta+\beta\mathbf{m}), \quad \theta \leftarrow \theta + \mathbf{m}$

**AdaGrad:** $\mathbf{s} \leftarrow \mathbf{s} + \nabla_\theta J(\theta) \otimes \nabla_\theta J(\theta), \quad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \nabla_\theta J(\theta) \oslash \sqrt{\mathbf{s}+\varepsilon}$

**RMSProp:** $\mathbf{s} \leftarrow \alpha\mathbf{s} + (1-\alpha)\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\otimes\nabla_\theta J(\theta), \quad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\oslash\sqrt{\mathbf{s}+\varepsilon}$

**Adam:**
$$\mathbf{m} \leftarrow \beta_1\mathbf{m} - (1-\beta_1)\nabla_\theta J(\theta), \qquad \mathbf{s} \leftarrow \beta_2\mathbf{s} + (1-\beta_2)\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\otimes\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$$
$$\hat{\mathbf{m}} \leftarrow \frac{\mathbf{m}}{1-\beta_1^{\,t}}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{s}} \leftarrow \frac{\mathbf{s}}{1-\beta_2^{\,t}}, \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta + \eta\hat{\mathbf{m}}\oslash\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{s}}+\varepsilon}$$

**Cosine annealing:** $\eta_t = \eta_{min} + \dfrac{1}{2}(\eta_{max}-\eta_{min})\left(1+\cos\left(\dfrac{t}{T_{max}}\pi\right)\right)$

**Max-norm constraint:** $\|\mathbf{w}\|_2 \le r$

## Questions and Answers

### 1. What problem do Glorot and He initialization aim to fix?

The vanishing/exploding gradients problem — specifically, its root cause in poorly-scaled random weight initialization. Left unchecked, naive initialization (e.g. plain unit-variance normal weights combined with a saturating activation like sigmoid) causes each layer's output variance to compound as it moves forward through the network, eventually saturating the upper layers' activations and leaving almost no usable gradient to propagate back down to the lower layers. Glorot/He initialization scale the initial random weights specifically so that a layer's output variance roughly matches its input variance (and, as much as possible, gradient variance is preserved flowing backward too), keeping signal and gradient magnitude stable through many stacked layers right from the start of training.

### 2. Is it OK to initialize all weights to the same randomly-chosen value, as long as that value came from He initialization?

No. The entire *point* of random initialization is to break the symmetry between neurons in the same layer (see the ANN chapter) — if every weight in a layer starts at the exact same value, every neuron in that layer computes the exact same output and receives the exact same gradient update forever, no matter how many neurons there are. Using He initialization's *variance formula* is about picking the right *scale* for the randomness, not about picking one specific value and reusing it everywhere; each weight still needs its own independently-sampled random value.

### 3. Is it OK to initialize bias terms to zero?

Yes, this is fine and standard practice — bias terms don't have the symmetry-breaking problem weights do (their gradients naturally differ across neurons because of the differing *weights*, even if all biases start identical), so there's no real benefit to randomizing them. Some people initialize biases the same way as weights anyway; it doesn't make a meaningful difference either way.

### 4. When would you want to use each activation function covered in this chapter?

ReLU is a strong, cheap general default for hidden layers in most networks. Leaky ReLU (or its randomized/parametric variants) is worth reaching for if you're seeing the dying-ReLU problem in practice. ELU and SELU trade some speed for smoothness and better gradient behavior — SELU specifically only when you can meet its fairly strict self-normalization conditions (plain stack of dense layers, standardized inputs, LeCun normal init, no other normalization/regularization tricks). GELU, Swish, Mish, and SwiGLU tend to outperform plain ReLU on genuinely complex tasks (Swish being a good complex-task default, with Mish/SwiGLU offering marginal further gains at extra compute cost; SwiGLU specifically shows up constantly in modern Transformers). For latency-sensitive deployments, favor cheap options like Leaky ReLU or ReLU² over the smoother, costlier functions.

### 5. What may happen if the SGD `momentum` hyperparameter is set too close to 1 (e.g. 0.99999)?

With almost no "friction" in the system, the momentum vector accumulates gradients over an enormous number of steps and can grow to a very large magnitude, causing the optimizer to badly overshoot the minimum, then swing back and overshoot again, oscillating for a very long time (or diverging outright) rather than settling down cleanly. Momentum needs enough decay (a $\beta$ meaningfully below 1, typically around 0.9) to actually damp these oscillations and let the optimizer converge.

### 6. Name three ways to produce a sparse model.

(1) Train a model normally, then zero out the smallest weights afterward (e.g. via `torch.nn.utils.prune.l1_unstructured()` for individual weights, or `prune.ln_structured()` to drop whole neurons/channels). (2) Apply strong $\ell_1$ regularization during training, which specifically pushes many weights toward exactly zero (unlike $\ell_2$, which shrinks weights but rarely zeroes them out). (3) Scale down the randomly initialized weights before training begins, which encourages more weights to end up near/at zero.

### 7. Does dropout slow down training? Inference? What about MC dropout?

Regular dropout does slow down *training* convergence noticeably (though it's usually worth it for the resulting generalization improvement) — but it's switched off entirely at inference time, so it adds essentially no extra cost to making predictions. MC dropout, by design, keeps dropout active at inference time and runs each input through the model multiple times to average the results — so unlike regular dropout, MC dropout genuinely *does* slow down inference, roughly in proportion to how many Monte Carlo samples (repeated forward passes) you use per prediction.

### 8. Train a deep MLP on CIFAR10, progressively adding techniques from this chapter — what actually happens at each step?

Following the book's exercise progression (20 hidden layers of 100 neurons each — deliberately far too deep/wide for this task, to make the effect of each technique visible):

*   **Baseline** (He/Kaiming init, Swish activation via `nn.SiLU`, no normalization, NAdam optimizer at `lr=2e-3`, early stopping): converged to roughly **38% validation accuracy** before early stopping kicked in, with training accuracy climbing well past validation accuracy — a clear overfitting signal for a network this oversized relative to the task.
*   **+ Batch normalization** (`nn.BatchNorm1d` after each hidden layer, same optimizer/rate): reached a noticeably better peak of roughly **40% validation accuracy**, and reached it in far fewer epochs (best result by epoch 6, versus epoch 15 for the baseline) — confirming BN's headline benefit of much faster convergence. Each individual epoch ran a bit slower due to the extra per-layer computation, but since so many fewer epochs were needed overall, total training time was still competitive. The model still clearly overfit afterward (training accuracy kept climbing toward 68% while validation accuracy stalled and drifted down), underscoring that batch norm is a *convergence* aid and a *mild* regularizer — not a fix for an architecture this oversized on its own.
*   **+ SELU self-normalization** (replacing Swish+BatchNorm with `nn.SELU`, LeCun normal initialization, and a standardization layer, since SELU needs standardized inputs; back to plain SGD since that's what the self-normalizing paper's setup assumes): this was the strongest single configuration on its own, reaching roughly **45–46% validation accuracy** — noticeably better than either the baseline or the batch-norm version, achieved without any explicit normalization layer at all, purely from SELU's self-normalizing property doing that job implicitly.
*   **+ Alpha dropout** (`nn.AlphaDropout(0.1)` layered onto the SELU architecture, NAdam at `lr=1e-3`): training became visibly noisier epoch to epoch (dropout's randomness shows up directly in the validation curve), landing in a similar-to-slightly-lower range of roughly **44–45% validation accuracy** compared to plain SELU alone in this run — regularizing an already-reasonably-fit model doesn't always show up as a clean accuracy win by itself; its real value is typically in reducing the overfitting gap and unlocking MC dropout as a follow-up technique on top of the same trained model, without needing to retrain.
*   **+ 1cycle scheduling** (same alpha-dropout/SELU architecture, `OneCycleLR` with `max_lr=1e-2` over a fixed 60-epoch budget): reached the best overall result across the whole exercise, roughly **46% validation accuracy**, and did so within a fixed epoch budget without triggering early stopping at all — consistent with 1cycle's reputation for reaching a strong result efficiently within a bounded number of epochs, rather than needing an open-ended, patience-based training run.

The overall shape of the results matches the chapter's core message directly: initialization and activation function choice alone (the baseline) only gets you so far; normalization (batch-norm or SELU's built-in self-normalization) meaningfully improves both convergence speed and final accuracy; and regularization plus a well-chosen learning rate schedule squeeze out further, if more modest, gains on top — with no single technique being a substitute for the others.
